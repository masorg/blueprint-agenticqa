### 5.3 Models cannot weaken the verdict (RQ3)

No hostile router, classifier, advisor, or pull-request text changed a verdict, and the model baseline shows what that is worth: a model that decided pass or fail from the same inputs approved {{injBaselineFalseApproves}} of {{injWithFailures}} failing runs ({{injBaselinePct}}%), mostly when told that failures were "known flaky". Hostile routers (all 32 ways of switching the five layers on or off) and hostile classifiers (six behaviours) ran in the real pipeline with every test selected. Before the two fixes in Table V, hostile routers and classifiers broke the verdict or the executed tests in {{advViolationsBefore}} of {{advCasesBefore}} cases on the demo services and {{advRealClickViolationsBefore}} of {{advRealClickCases}} on click. After the fixes, none of {{advCasesAfter}} demo-service cases (95% upper bound {{advUpperBoundPct}}%) and none of {{realCases}} cases on {{realSuitesRan}} real projects broke. With the fixed design this follows from the verdict rule (Section 3.4), so the attack checks the implementation, and the runs without the fixes show that it finds such bugs.

Eight hostile advisors ran on mutants of {{selAtkProjects}} projects ({{selAtkCases}} cases). The core tests failed to run in {{selAtkM1}} cases and the verdict was weaker than without advice in {{selAtkM2}}; {{selAtkTimeouts}} runs that hit the time limit were excluded. A gpt-4o-mini advisor added {{selAdvMeanAdded}} tests per change at about {{selAdvTokens}} tokens and found no failing test outside the core, so a model advisor is safe but, in these projects, not needed.

Seven kinds of misleading text, after [2], added to every demo scenario on four models ({{injRuns}} runs), changed the verdict {{injPipelineFlips}} times and approved {{injPipelineFalseApproves}} of the {{injWithFailures}} runs with a failing test (95% upper bound {{injGateUpperPct}}%). They changed the router's choice in {{injRoutingFlips}} of {{injRuns}} runs, and ordinary text from real pull requests changed it about as often ({{noisePooledPct}}%): the router is easily moved, which is why it has no power over the verdict.

With every model replaced by its rule, on {{nmN}} scenarios of the demo services, the rules gave the right verdict in {{nmStubVerdict}}, as did every model, and named exactly the failing layers in {{nmStubLayers}}, against {{nmModelLayersMin}} to {{nmModelLayersMax}} for the models. On {{routCommits}} real commits, a fixed check of the project's dependencies recognised agentic projects better ({{routAccManifest}}%) than any model ({{routAccMistralSmall}}% to {{routAccMini}}%). Models improve grouping and explanation, at a cost in time and tokens, and add nothing to safety.

### 5.4 The test pyramid (RQ5)

The pyramid groups results by failure class, so that a reviewer sees which kind of failure a change causes; it is not a detection mechanism. On the {{mutValid}} mutants of the demo services, all layers together killed {{mutRateFull}}% against {{mutRateUnit}}% for unit tests alone, and the agentic layers killed almost none on their own (trajectory {{mutUniqueTrajectory}}, LLM-judge {{mutUniqueJudge}}), because small code changes rarely cause the failures they target. Real agent frameworks rarely test those failures yet: {{agScanCue}} of {{agScanFiles}} test files in {{agScanProjects}} frameworks are named like trajectory or judge tests.
